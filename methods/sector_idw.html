Sector inverse distance weighting

The value at a location is a weighted average of nearby survey points, each weighted by the inverse of its distance raised to a power \(\alpha\) (Zelenskyi et al., 2020; Zelensky and Donchenko, 2025, eq. 11):

\[Z_A = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} Z_i\, d_i^{-\alpha}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} d_i^{-\alpha}}\]

Plain inverse distance weighting has a known weakness: a cluster of points on one side of the location dominates the average even though the cluster carries little extra information. The sector rule fixes this. A search circle of the given radius is divided into n_sectors equal angular sectors and only the single closest point in each sector is used, so at most n_sectors points contribute and no direction dominates. A location with no survey point inside the radius returns NaN.

Anisotropy

The critical correlation radius \(r_k\) (the lag at which the spatial autocorrelation of the measured quantity dies out) can differ by direction, for example along versus across the strike of an ore body. Passing anisotropy=(rk_x, rk_y) scales distances by \(r_{k,\max} / r_{k,i}\) before weighting, so a point along the better-correlated axis counts as closer (Zelensky and Donchenko, 2025, eqs. 12-13).

from pitmap import SectorIDW
model = SectorIDW(radius=250, power=2, n_sectors=8).fit(xy, z)
z_hat = model.predict(grid_xy)

# along-strike correlation radius 40 m, across-strike 10 m
model = SectorIDW(radius=250, anisotropy=(40, 10)).fit(xy, z)

Parameters

radius

Search radius, in the units of xy.

power

The exponent \(\alpha\). 2 is the usual choice.

n_sectors

Number of angular sectors. 8 follows the original system. With 1 the method reduces to nearest neighbour.

anisotropy

(rk_x, rk_y) or None for isotropic distances.
