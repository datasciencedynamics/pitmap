Polynomial surface

A bivariate polynomial of degree \(M\) fitted to the survey points by least squares (Zelensky and Donchenko, 2025):

\[Z(x, y) = \sum_{i + j \le M} a_{ij}\, x^i y^j\]

The original system solves the normal equations by Gaussian elimination; pitmap centres and scales the coordinates and solves the least-squares problem directly, which is the same estimate with better conditioning at high degree.

Adequacy

A polynomial of high enough degree passes through every control point with \(R^2 = 1\) and is still useless, because it oscillates between the points. The 2025 paper therefore judges a fit by three indicators, all available as attributes after fit:

r2_

Coefficient of determination. 0.5 to 0.8 satisfactory, above 0.8 highly reliable, below 0.5 ineffective.

variance_ratio_

\(k = (\sigma^2_{data} - \sigma^2_{surface}) / \sigma^2_{data} \cdot 100\). The surface should reproduce the spread of the data; the paper accepts \(|k| < 20\%\).

lag1_autocorrelation_

\(r_1\), the autocorrelation between adjacent values of the input sequence. Above 0.7 the data carry a consistent trend worth modelling; below, they behave as independent values and no smooth surface will fit well. This describes the data, not the fit, and depends on the order of the points.

is_adequate() applies the first two thresholds; select_degree fits degrees 0 to max_degree and returns the lowest adequate one.

from pitmap import PolynomialSurface, select_degree

model = PolynomialSurface(degree=3).fit(xy, z)
model.adequacy()      # {'r2': ..., 'variance_ratio': ..., 'lag1_autocorrelation': ...}
z_hat = model.predict(grid_xy)

best = select_degree(xy, z, max_degree=5)
best.degree

Parameters

degree

Polynomial degree. The original system offers 0 to 5.
